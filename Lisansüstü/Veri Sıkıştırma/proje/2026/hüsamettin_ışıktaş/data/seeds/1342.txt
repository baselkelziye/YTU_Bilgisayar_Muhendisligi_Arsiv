*** START OF THE PROJECT GUTENBERG EBOOK PRIDE AND PREJUDICE ***

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.
However little known the feelings or views of such a man may be on his first entering a neighbourhood,
this truth is so well fixed in the minds of the surrounding families, that he is considered as the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters.

"My dear Mr. Bennet," said his lady to him one day, "have you heard that Netherfield Park is let at last?"
Mr. Bennet replied that he had not.
"But it is," returned she; "for Mrs. Long has just been here, and she told me all about it."
Mr. Bennet made no answer.
"Do not you want to know who has taken it?" cried his wife impatiently.
"You want to tell me, and I have no objection to hearing it."

This was invitation enough.
The narrative of family visits, social expectations, and the tiny negotiations of pride and kindness
continued over breakfast tables, carriage rides, and evening gatherings, where each sentence could carry
the weight of an inheritance and each glance could be interpreted in four contradictory ways.

By and by, what began as neighborhood curiosity turned into a larger story about judgement:
how quickly people decide, how slowly they revise, and how often they confuse confidence with insight.
In this movement from first impressions to better understanding, characters learn that wit without empathy
is brittle, and dignity without openness becomes another form of fear.

Their world is full of letters, walks, parlors, and dances; it appears quiet on the surface,
yet every conversation rearranges social possibility. One family waits for invitations,
another evaluates alliances, while a third places too much faith in rank and too little in character.
Still, beneath every formal exchange, there persists a practical moral question:
what does it mean to recognize another person accurately, and what does it cost to admit we were wrong?

The answer comes slowly, through embarrassment, refusal, apology, and renewed effort.
Where vanity had demanded applause, humility begins to ask better questions.
Where misunderstanding had frozen affection, clearer speech starts to restore trust.
In the end, affection proves less theatrical than expected; it is expressed not only in declarations,
but in attention, consistency, and the difficult work of changing one's mind.
